Find a starting point for a workplace situation

Choose a situation to see a practical next step and wording you could use.

When two requests both come first

A colleague asks for slides by midday while you are finishing a report due at the same time.

A starting point. Write down what you can finish by midday and who can decide which piece moves. Ask for that decision before trying to do both.

“I can send the report or the slides by midday. Which one does the team need first?”

When the brief changes halfway through

You have drafted a briefing for the project team. It now needs to go to the board.

A starting point. Separate what you can reuse from what needs a decision. Keep the figures you have checked and ask what the new audience needs to decide.

“I can reuse the analysis. What decision does the board need from this paper, and is Thursday still the deadline?”

Prepare a specific request

Two deadlines moved in meetings you did not attend. You want to raise this with your manager.

A starting point. Pick one instance and describe its effect. Ask how the change was communicated, then agree where future changes will be recorded.

“I was working to Friday because that was the date in the brief. How did the new date reach the team? Could we update the brief when a deadline moves?”

Leave yourself a way back into the task

Messages keep interrupting you while you check a spreadsheet.

A starting point. Choose one check, agree where urgent questions should go, and write down where you stopped before switching away.

Restart note: “Rows 1 to 12 checked. Next, compare row 13 with the source invoice.”

Work out what can be repaired

You discover an old figure in a briefing you have already circulated.

A starting point. Identify the figure, check whether it changes the recommendation, and tell the people relying on it what you will correct and when.

“The total on page two came from last month’s file. I am checking the recommendation and will send a corrected version by 11:00.”

Propose a change you can try

Decisions come quickly at a weekly meeting, and you have more to contribute once you have reviewed the papers.

A starting point. Describe the barrier, suggest one change, and agree when to review it. You can explain what helps without sharing a diagnosis.

“Could we circulate the questions the day before the next two meetings? Then I can check the figures and bring a considered response.”

Make room for the point you want to raise

A meeting invite says “project update”, and you are unsure what to prepare.

A starting point. Ask which decision is needed. Once you know, jot down the evidence and one question you want to bring.

“Are we choosing a supplier or reviewing progress? If we are choosing, I will bring the cost comparison.”

Include the work after the first draft

You have been asked to send a finished paper on Friday and a colleague needs time to review it.

A starting point. Work back from Friday to allow for the review and revisions. Use time tracked on similar work to estimate the first draft.

“If I get the draft to you on Wednesday, could you return comments by Thursday lunchtime?”

Attach one action to an existing event

You often leave a meeting knowing your next action, then lose it when another call starts.

A starting point. Try adding one action to your task list as you close the meeting notes. After a few meetings, check whether that cue works for you.

Example task: “Email Priya the revised figures by Thursday at 10:00.”

Choose one question that reduces uncertainty

You have been added to a project channel and cannot tell whether anyone needs something from you.

A starting point. Write down what you know, what is unclear, and who can answer. Use their response to decide whether to add a task.

“Am I here to follow progress, or is there a particular decision you would like my input on?”

You are going on leave tomorrow. “Prepare the handover” is on your list, but it is difficult to tell where to begin. Try ticking off these example steps.

Pick up a task after an interruption

A call ran over and you missed a planned slot. You have checked the spending figures for your report. Writing the explanation is still open.

Keep a smaller step for today

You have 20 minutes before the end of the day. Move “Explain the largest change in spending” into that slot. Leave proofreading as a separate step.

Restart note. The largest increase is in travel costs. The figures are checked. Start with the reason for the change.

Protect time for the unfinished step

Move the explanation to a free slot tomorrow morning. Keep the figures marked as checked so you can pick up where you stopped.

If someone is waiting. “The figures are ready today. I can send them now and follow with the explanation tomorrow morning. Does that work for you?”

Review note. Advance notice helped, but sending the agenda on the morning of the meeting left little time to respond.

Clarify a request from a teammate

A teammate writes, “Could you look at this before the meeting?” Choose what you need to clarify to see how you might reply.

Clarify the scope

“I can review it. Would you like me to check the figures, comment on the recommendation, or proofread the text?”

Agree a time

“What time would you like my comments? I can send notes by 14:00, or flag the main question sooner if you need it for the meeting.”

Make the trade-off explicit

“I am finishing the client summary this morning. Which would you like me to prioritise if there is only time for one before the meeting?”

Interactive example

Build an inclusive hiring pack

A hiring team is preparing to recruit an analyst. Explore the six parts of its example pack to see what the team could agree before a candidate reaches each stage.

Define the work

Example. The analyst will compare monthly results and explain the main changes in a written briefing.

Preparation check. Confirm which skills are essential on day one and which can be learned in the role.

Set expectations

Example. The invitation gives the interview length, format, names of the panel members, and time set aside for questions.

Preparation check. Make sure the invitation and calendar entry give the same timings.

Agree how evidence will be gathered

Example. Each candidate receives the same core question about interpreting a dataset. The panel agrees what evidence to look for before interviews begin.

Preparation check. Give each panel member the questions, scoring guidance, and their role in the discussion.

Describe the expected output

Example. Review a short dataset and submit a one-page summary of two findings. The brief states how long to spend and which tools may be used.

Preparation check. Ask someone unfamiliar with the task to identify the output, time allowance, and submission route from the brief.

Name a point of contact

Example. The invitation names a recruiting contact for candidates who want to discuss a change to the interview or assessment format.

Preparation check. Agree who monitors the route, who follows up, and what the panel needs to know.

Prepare for the first week

Example. The recruiting lead and line manager agree who will send the first-week schedule, equipment details, and the name of a contact for questions.

Preparation check. Confirm with the incoming colleague what information they want passed on and to whom.
